How interest rates work

What sets your interest rate?

Think of it as three layers stacked on top of each other.

The safe baseline

The 10-year Treasury rate. It is what the U.S. government pays to borrow money. Lending to the government is about the safest loan there is, so it is the starting line.

+

The gap

Called the spread. A home loan is riskier and more work than lending to the government, so investors ask for extra. Normally about 1.5 to 2 points.

+

Your details

Your down payment, your credit, and any points you pay nudge your rate up or down.

=

Your rate on today's quote. Not to scale.

Who sets the baseline?

Nobody does alone. Investors buy and sell Treasuries every day. If they expect prices to rise fast or the economy to run hot, they ask for more and the rate goes up. If they get nervous and want safety, it drops. The Federal Reserve and how much the government borrows nudge it too.

Why is there a gap?

Imagine lending a friend $10. If your friend always pays on time, you ask for a tiny thank-you. If they might pay you back early, forget, or need someone to keep track of it, you ask for more. Lenders face the same three things.

What is in the gap?

Early payoff: you can pay off or refinance anytime, so when rates fall the investor gets money back at the worst time. Missed payments: an insurance fee of about 0.4 points covers that. Work: someone has to create, package and manage the loan. When markets are jumpy, the cushion grows.

We can't control the baseline or the gap. What we can control is how much of your payment you carry in the first years.

Sources: Federal Reserve Bank of Boston, May 2026, which puts the gap at roughly 1 to 3 points since 2000; National Mortgage News, which describes a normal gap of 1.5 to 2 points.

Using concessions well

Recent deals have closed with up to 6% in seller concessions

In this market, a concession can be worth more to you than a lower price. Here is how we think about it.

Ask for credits, not only a lower price

A price cut trims your payment a little, forever. A credit pays your costs up front and can lower your payment in the first years, when a high rate hurts most.

Price and credits can work together

Sometimes a slightly higher price with a bigger credit nets out better for the buyer, as long as the home still appraises at the contract price.

Your agent leads the offer

Your agent writes and negotiates the offer. Our job is to run the numbers quickly so you both know what a credit is worth, and to confirm it fits your loan.
